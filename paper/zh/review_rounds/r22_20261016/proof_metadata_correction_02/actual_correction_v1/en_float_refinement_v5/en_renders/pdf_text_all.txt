Abstract
Timing closure has become a critical challenge in digital chip design as circuit
scale and design complexity continue to grow. Engineering change order (ECO)
techniques reduce the iteration cost of re-synthesis and physical implementation by
applying localized modifications to an existing design. Among them, pre-placement
gate-level timing ECO explores local netlist modifications before placement and
routing, but its candidate space is limited and per-candidate evaluation is costly.
FAECO is a framework for candidate exploration and failure characterization. It
builds and ranks four types of local modification candidates—logic-equivalent
replacement (R), gate sizing (G), buffer insertion (B), and joint modification
(JOINT)—over the local logic around violating paths via a multi-feature weighted
cut and a critical-path cover cut, characterizes candidate failures into six types used
for search diagnosis and ablation analysis, and commits candidates round by round
through function and structural checks, timing analysis and final equivalence check-
ing. Its core idea is to organize the multi-type candidate space and the evaluation
order so as to improve search efficiency under a limited verification budget. On IS-
CAS89, ITC-99 and PicoRV32, 8/8, 18/19 and 2/2 instances achieve pre-placement
ideal-net WNS improvement (the third PicoRV32 module has no setup path and
is excluded), with no regression on the rest. Under fixed weights and 20 rounds,
mixed candidates improve WNS by 1.07 ns on average versus 0.16 ns for gate sizing

1. Introduction
1.1. Background and problem
Timing closure is a major challenge in digital chip design as integrated circuits
become larger and more complex. Engineering change order (ECO) techniques
apply local modifications to an existing design, avoiding complete re-synthesis
and physical implementation and reducing iteration cost. ECO can target either
functional errors or timing violations. This work addresses gate-level timing ECO
after synthesis and before placement and routing. A gate-level local modification
applies an equivalent-cell replacement, gate sizing, buffer insertion, or a small
combination of gate modifications within the fanin cone of violating logic in an
already mapped netlist. Conventional timing ECO mainly uses buffer insertion (B)
and gate sizing (G) to change driving capability and path load, improving worst
negative slack (WNS) and total negative slack (TNS). For severe path violations,
the capability of B/G optimization is limited, and structural remapping remains
constrained by the available candidate space[1–3]. The increasing cost of post-
placement modifications makes early ECO exploration more valuable. However,
physical information is unavailable before placement, and systematic choices of
what to modify and in what order to evaluate the alternatives remain difficult.
Beyond B/G optimization, technology remapping and local logic patches ex-
pand the space of structural changes. These methods can change the local logic
implementation but require additional functional-equivalence checks and candidate
screening. The interaction between patch construction and physical optimization
affects both verification cost and timing benefit.
Pre-placement gate-level timing ECO faces two related limitations: restricted
candidate types and costly candidate evaluation. B/G modifications mainly change
driving capability and load, while structural remapping needs an effective way
to organize and rank many alternatives. Three questions follow: how to expand
the local modification space for deep logic, how to control functional, timing
and physical validation cost, and how to characterize candidate failures so that

they inform search diagnosis. FAECO addresses candidate expansion through the
exploration framework in Section 3.3, evaluation cost through layered validation in
Section 3.6, and search analysis through typed failure characterization in Section 3.5.
We define candidate exploration as the generation, screening and ranking of
multiple types of local modification around violating logic windows. It organizes
alternative structural changes and prioritizes candidates with timing potential under
a limited validation budget. The central problem is therefore how to organize
multiple local modification types and allocate finite validation resources effectively
when physical information is incomplete.
1.2. Motivation
This work investigates how to construct additional local modification candidates
within violating fanin cones and use critical-path information to arrange their
evaluation order. Many candidates fail functional, structural or timing checks during
the search. These failures characterize limitations of the current candidate space
and ranking. FAECO uses a multi-feature weighted cut and a critical-path cover
cut to generate candidates, and classifies validation failures to support subsequent
search analysis.
The experiments use ISCAS89[4], ITC-99[5] and PicoRV322 netlists mapped to
the public SKY130 HD standard-cell library. Ideal-net static timing analysis (STA)
measures candidate timing benefit. Selected experiments also apply a simplified
Standard Parasitic Exchange Format (SPEF) model, and independent sequential
equivalence checking (SEC) evaluates the final repaired netlists.
1.3. Contributions
The work centers on candidate exploration for pre-placement gate-level timing
ECO:
1. A candidate exploration framework. FAECO takes local windows around
violating paths, constructs R/G/B/JOINT candidates using weighted and
critical-path cover cuts, and combines cut cost, critical-path coverage and
strategy priority to rank them. The design expands local modification al-
ternatives and aims to reduce candidate evaluation cost in the absence of
placement information.
2. Failure characterization and search analysis. Six failure classes cover
local function/structure, boundary closure, candidate size, insufficient timing

2. Related work
2.1. Structural remapping and logic patches
ECO research includes structural remapping, logic-patch construction, and
B/G physical optimization. Structural remapping changes local cells and their
implementation to improve timing. Some methods combine remapping with buffer
insertion and sizing[1], while symbolic sampling explores rectification points[6].
FAECO uses Liberty function matching to screen replaceable cells before timing
evaluation, and performs global SEC independently. Spare-cell allocation in[1] uses
post-route load and spare-cell placement, whereas FAECO constructs candidates
before placement. Symbolic sampling in[6] expands the search space and uses
validation results to screen rectification points.
Logic-patch methods typically extract a local window around a target rectifica-
tion point, represent candidate logic with functionally reduced and-inverter graphs
(FRAIGs), and use Boolean satisfiability (SAT) proofs to select functionally equiv-
alent patches for functional repair[7, 8]. Subsequent work incorporates resource
cost into patch generation and uses SAT or combinational equivalence checking
(CEC) for validation[8, 9]. FAECO brings computable local function-preservation
information into candidate ranking and uses ideal-net timing as a separate accep-
tance signal. When equivalence validation fails, representative patch flows expand
the window or patch size and enumerate again[10], increasing construction and
validation cost.
Recent B/G physical optimization includes gate sizing, joint B/G optimization
and physically aware approaches[2, 3, 11, 12]. Learning-based methods further
improve efficiency[3, 12]. They primarily change driving capability and network
load and generally do not reconstruct Boolean functions. FAECO constructs several
local candidate types in violating fanin cones of mapped netlists and ranks them
using critical-path information.

2.2. B/G physical optimization and positioning
Several related approaches use commercial EDA engines or internal industrial
flows. We use public Yosys3, OpenSTA4 and OpenROAD[13] tools to implement
gate-level candidate search on SKY130 HD5. The flow covers mapped netlists,
candidate construction, ideal-net STA, and post-placement/global-route parasitic
estimation. Sequential experiments apply construction-level state-preservation
checks, followed by independent whole-netlist SEC.
Structural remapping and B/G physical optimization are commonly used for
physical adjustments after placement or during routing; logic-patch construction
often performs functional repair between synthesis and placement. FAECO com-
bines local modification of a mapped netlist with pre-placement timing screening,

evaluation goals impede direct quantitative comparisons, so the experiments use
a common public mapping and constraint environment to investigate candidate

3. Method
3.1. Problem definition
Given a mapped netlist G′, target period T, timing constraints and cell library
L, FAECO searches local candidates p in fanin cones of violating endpoints to
produce G∗. The main timing acceptance condition is ∆WNS(p) > 0. Independent
SEC compares the final repaired netlist with the baseline. Optional physical gating
adds the SPEF condition in Section 3.6. Optional TNS-aware acceptance allows
equal WNS with improved TNS.
The four transformations are R, a cross-equivalent-cell or logic-implementation
replacement excluding pure drive-size changes; G, a drive-size change within
the same functional cell family; B, an identity buffer insertion; and JOINT, joint
enumeration. Sequential experiments do not modify flip-flops, clock or reset cells.
A candidate comprises local gates and boundary ports and is generated only in
combinational regions without tri-state, clock-gating or reset logic. Library function
matching and structural rules screen constructions before final SEC. Table 3 defines
the main notation.
3.2. Overview
FAECO has three stages (Fig. 1):
1. Mapping preparation. A two-step Yosys flow produces a SKY130 HD
mapped netlist G′. Liberty provides cell functions and timing. Berkeley
Logic Interchange Format (BLIF) normalization and ABC cec[14] support
combinational checks.

3.3.1. Local window extraction
As illustrated in Fig. 2, Cw determines candidate boundaries, node cost guides
selection/ranking within the boundary, and Cc supplements critical-path coverage.
Reverse traversal from a violating endpoint extracts a local combinational fanin
cone C(G, t). Gate annotations include R availability, critical-path coverage and
candidate-size features.
3.3.2. Candidate boundaries from complementary sources
Weighted minimum-cut cost combines boundary, candidate-size, verification-
cost and fanout features. For gates with R candidates, λc introduces the depth
discount in Eq. (2). The same weight adjusts ranking of complementary critical-
path cover candidates. R availability controls candidate instantiation and strategy
switching: when a gate has no R equivalent, its R-related coverage discount and

fanout term are disabled and the search turns to G/B. The weighted boundary Cw
prioritizes local scale, while Cc covers timing-critical gates.

default weights are used without tuning by circuit or dataset.

3.6. Validation and acceptance
Local function and structure checks screen candidates before OpenSTA tim-
ing evaluation. R candidates use Liberty matching; G/B use function-preserving
constructions. ABC cec can validate combinational netlists when enabled. The
main and cross-dataset experiments apply independent SEC to each final base-
line/repaired pair, separately from ideal-net candidate acceptance. Under SKY130
behavioral cell models, SEC checks combinational logic and state-transition rela-
tionships. OpenSTA measures ideal-net timing; the OpenROAD flow measures
timing with global-route parasitic estimates.

3.7. Construction complexity
The split graph uses max-flow/min-cut. For n cone gates and f dependency
edges, there are V = 2n+2 nodes and E = O(n+f) edges. Edmonds–Karp costs
O(V E2) per cut. This excludes candidate STA and final SEC, which dominate
practical runtime; the individual cut solve contributes little. Reverse breadth-first
search extracts C(G, t). Large violating cones are partitioned by depth into sub-
cones bounded by gate count, with independently generated candidates ranked
along critical paths to control memory and solve size.

4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
Environment and datasets. Experiments ran in WSL2 (Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS;
kernel 6.6.87.2-microsoft-standard-WSL2) on an AMD Ryzen 7 7435H host, with
16 visible logical cores and 15 GB memory. The main flow uses OSS-CAD Suite
(Yosys 0.67+146, OpenSTA 3.1.0); SEC uses WSL Yosys 0.33. Datasets com-
prise eight ISCAS89 circuits, 19 ITC-99 circuits and three PicoRV32 modules.
ISCAS89 and ITC-99 circuits use a 0.5 ns clock period; the PicoRV32 core, multi-
plier and register bank use 5, 2 and 0.5 ns, respectively. WNS improvement is the
primary outcome, with the baseline and repaired netlist of each circuit evaluated
under identical constraints. Other metrics are global-route-estimated WNS and
candidate STA counts; TNS supports optional auxiliary acceptance. b06 retains
equal-WNS/improved-TNS acceptance, which is excluded from strict-WNS im-
provement counts. TNS-aware acceptance is not combined with physical gating in
these experiments.
Inclusion. ISCAS89 includes eight sequential circuits that map successfully
through the common Yosys-to-SKY130 flow and have valid setup paths. ITC-
99 includes b01–b15, b17 and b20–b22. b18 and b19 are separately reported
large examples, outside the unified 19-circuit statistics. PicoRV32 includes its
core, picorv32_pcpi_mul multiplier, and storage-dominated picorv32_regs,
covering control, arithmetic and storage structures.
Configurations. Section 4.2 uses one round and stops after the first improve-
ment; Section 4.3 permits eight rounds. Section 4.4 compares strategy configura-
tions under a 20-round limit and separately tests feedback. OpenROAD re-evaluates
the accepted candidates from Section 4.2; SPEF scans/gating are separate experi-

4.3. Cross-dataset evaluation
4.3.1. ITC-99
Eighteen of the 19 unified ITC-99 circuits obtain strict WNS gain, while b06
remains unchanged; none regresses. At the default 60 s per-candidate limit, b17
candidates time out as F5. Increasing only b17’s limit to 180 s yields +0.38 ns
within 104 candidate evaluation records, accepting a G replacement from nor4b_1
to nor4b_2. The larger included circuits b14/b15/b17/b20/b21/b22 all improve. b06
has baseline WNS=-0.56 ns and several adjacent or4b_1 gates on its critical path.
In this R/G/B/JOINT search, G replacements are constrained by input-capacitance
changes, and B/R do not yield an effective gain. No strict WNS improvement is
observed in 664 candidate evaluation records.
G dominates accepted modifications: 17 circuits contain G acceptances (69 in
total), while B and R occur in 14 and ten circuits. b20 and b21 have the largest gains,
+1.98 and +2.75 ns, mainly from G with additional R modifications. The 19-circuit
set contains 3363 candidate evaluation records, of which 2994 STA results can be
independently traced to unique candidate reports (2890 in the 18-circuit archive and
104 recovered for b17). This is an identifiable result count; the historical archives
do not record all timeout launches, baseline analyses or cone refreshes. Median
and mean gain are +0.18 and +0.43 ns; b20/b21, whose baseline WNS is at most
-13 ns, account for much of the difference. Table 12 reports failure triggers and
provides search-diagnostic evidence.
Two separate large examples are outside these 19-circuit statistics. Mapped b18
has 75,700 cells approximately, with WNS changing from -13.34 to -13.27 ns at
0.5 ns in 24 candidate-level STA measurements. b19 has approximately 151,000
cells, changing from -17.44 to -16.00 ns in 59 measurements.

4.4. Candidate-space, ranking and feedback comparisons
The first experiment uses an archived critical-instance strategy-enumeration
runner under a 20-pass limit (Table 9), with first-improvement cost in Table 10.
It enumerates candidates and submits changes instance by instance within a pass,
without the weighted or cover cuts in Eq. (2). The mixed configuration uses auxil-
iary TNS acceptance and R/G/B/JOINT; the randomized configuration enumerates
R/G/B only. Their differences therefore reflect the joint effects of candidate con-
struction and evaluation order.
With fixed search weights, mixed strategies average 1.07 ns gain versus 0.16 ns
for G alone, 0.54 ns for B alone and 0.40 ns for the three randomized seeds. The
shared 20-pass limit does not imply equal actual STA consumption. In six of seven
circuits with ledgers, the mixed strategy reaches its first gain before the random-
seed mean; s713 is the exception. First-improvement cost varies by up to sevenfold
between seeds. The comparison describes strategy-level outcomes and startup cost;
separate controls in the same runner are still needed to isolate weighted and cover
cuts.

5. Conclusion
FAECO constructs R/G/B/JOINT candidates through weighted and critical-path
cover cuts, combining failure characterization, iterative submission and layered
validation for local pre-placement timing optimization. In the fixed-weight strategy
study, mixed candidates average 1.07 ns WNS gain versus 0.16 ns for G alone.
Cross-dataset evaluation demonstrates gains across multiple circuit families. In
archived strategy comparisons, mixed candidates reach first improvement earlier
than randomized strategies on most circuits, reflecting the joint effects of candidate
construction and evaluation order.
EMA does not add observed benefit over fixed weights, and expanded S can-
didates do not improve WNS despite reducing some mapped depths. After Open-
ROAD placement/global-route estimation, five circuits retain gains, one is un-
changed and two regress by at most 0.02 ns. Insufficient timing gain dominates
recorded failures (F4: 60.2% of evaluations), providing direction for candidate-
space design. Within these experiments, organizing the candidate space and evalua-
tion strategy offers a route to better pre-placement ECO outcomes beyond reliance
on a single modification type. The public-tool implementation provides a basis for
reproducible study of automated ECO search.
